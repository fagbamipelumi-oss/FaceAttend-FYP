## 2.1 Conceptual Review

### 2.1.1 Face Detection, Face Recognition, and Face Verification

These three terms are often used loosely in everyday speech but describe distinct computational tasks. Face detection is the task of locating where a face appears within an image or video frame, typically returning a bounding box, without any attempt to determine whose face it is. Face recognition is a broader term covering the task of determining a person's identity from a face image, which can be approached either as identification, where the system searches a full gallery of enrolled identities to decide who a face belongs to, or as verification, where the system checks a single specific claim, such as "does this face belong to the enrolled person with this identity" (El Fadel, 2025). The system described in this study performs detection first, to locate a face within a captured frame, and then performs a form of identification, comparing the detected face against every enrolled identity's stored samples rather than checking a single claimed identity.

### 2.1.2 Convolutional Neural Networks in Face Recognition

Modern face recognition systems are built on convolutional neural networks (CNNs), a class of neural network architecture in which layers of learned filters are applied across an image to build up increasingly abstract representations, from edges and textures in early layers to face-specific structure in deeper layers. El Fadel (2025) notes that this shift from hand-crafted feature extraction to learned CNN-based features is the central development that has driven the accuracy improvements face recognition has seen over the past decade. Rather than being trained to output a class label directly, a face recognition CNN is typically trained to output an embedding, described next, which is what makes it possible to recognize people the network never saw during training.

### 2.1.3 Face Embeddings

A face embedding is a fixed-length numerical vector, commonly 128 or 512 numbers, produced by passing a face image through a trained CNN. The network is trained so that embeddings of the same person's face land close together in this vector space while embeddings of different people's faces land far apart. Once a network is trained this way, recognizing a new, previously unseen person no longer requires retraining the network at all; it only requires computing that person's embedding from a few reference photographs and storing it, then later comparing a new embedding against the stored one by a distance measure. This is the mechanism this project relies on directly: enrollment computes and stores an embedding per accepted photograph, and recognition computes a fresh embedding from a live capture and searches for the closest stored embedding by Euclidean distance.

### 2.1.4 Transfer Learning

Transfer learning is the practice of taking a network already trained on a large, general dataset and reusing it for a new, related task rather than training a new network from randomly initialized weights. In face recognition specifically, this typically means reusing a face embedding network that has already been trained on a large face dataset, and simply enrolling new people by computing their embeddings with that already-trained network. This project relies entirely on transfer learning in this sense: it uses the `face_recognition` Python library's pretrained dlib ResNet encoder rather than training any network from scratch, which would not have been realistic within a final year project's data or time constraints.

## 2.2 Theoretical Review

The specific training objective that makes face embeddings useful is a deep metric learning approach, most notably the triplet loss introduced by Schroff et al. (2015) in their FaceNet architecture. Rather than training a network to output the absolute correct class label for a face, as an ordinary image classifier would, a triplet loss trains the network on triplets of images, an anchor image, a positive image of the same person, and a negative image of a different person, and adjusts the network's weights so that the distance between the anchor and positive embeddings shrinks while the distance between the anchor and negative embeddings grows. Schroff et al. (2015) reported that this relative, distance-based objective, rather than an absolute classification objective, was what allowed their network to generalize to identities never seen during training, which is precisely the property an attendance system's enrollment process depends on: every enrolled student is, by definition, a person the underlying encoder never saw during its own training.

This theoretical grounding also explains why a simple distance threshold is sufficient for the recognition decision this project makes. Because the network was trained so that same-person distances and different-person distances are pushed apart during training, a single decision rule, accept a match if the closest stored embedding's distance falls below a chosen threshold and reject it otherwise, is a direct and theoretically motivated consequence of the triplet-loss training objective, rather than an arbitrary rule invented for this project. The practical difficulty, and the reason this project treats its own decision threshold as something to be measured rather than assumed, is that the theory does not specify where exactly that threshold should sit for a new, previously unseen enrolled population and a new camera setup; that is an empirical question, addressed through the evaluation methodology.

## 2.3 Review of Related Works

**Class Attendance System Based on Face Recognition** (Alniemi & Mahmood, 2023) combined a Haar Cascade classifier for face detection with a FaceNet-based embedding network for recognition, comparing embeddings by Manhattan distance. The authors built a custom dataset of 3,900 images from 130 students captured from five angles under three lighting conditions, and reported single-face detection accuracy of 99.5% and an overall attendance-registration accuracy of 97.5%. The study did not report separate False Acceptance Rate or False Rejection Rate figures, and its high reported accuracy came from a dataset collected under controlled, cooperative conditions rather than an adversarial test that deliberately included unenrolled individuals.

**Facial Recognition Attendance Monitoring System using Deep Learning Techniques** (Thalor & Gaikwad, 2024) described an attendance system in which a captured classroom image is processed by a deep-learning-based detector before the detected faces are verified against a stored student database, with attendance then logged automatically. The authors positioned their system primarily against the inefficiency of manual and semi-automated attendance methods, arguing for automation on the basis of speed rather than presenting a detailed breakdown of recognition error rates, which limits how directly its reported performance can be compared against a metric-driven evaluation.

**Liveness Detection in Computer Vision: Transformer-based Self-Supervised Learning for Face Anti-Spoofing** (Keresh & Shamoi, 2024) applied a self-supervised transformer architecture to the task of distinguishing a live face capture from a spoofed presentation, such as a printed photograph or a screen replay, without relying on labeled spoof data during pretraining. The study is representative of a broader recent trend toward learned, data-driven liveness detection rather than the simple heuristic checks, such as requiring a blink or head movement, that this project uses. The tradeoff is complexity: a transformer-based liveness model is considerably heavier to train, deploy, and justify within a final year project than a heuristic check computed directly from facial landmark positions.

**Deep Learning Models for Robust Facial Liveness Detection** (Kuznetsov et al., 2025) surveyed and evaluated several deep learning approaches to facial liveness detection and explicitly noted that no evaluated method achieved complete robustness against every category of spoofing attack, a finding the present study treats as a reason to describe its own liveness mechanism honestly as a basic anti-spoofing measure rather than a security-grade guarantee.

**Facial Recognition Algorithms: A Systematic Literature Review** (El Fadel, 2025) reviewed the broader landscape of face recognition algorithms and their reported performance, and is used in this study primarily as a conceptual reference for the detection-versus-recognition-versus-verification distinction described earlier, rather than as a single implemented system to compare against.

**Facial Recognition Performance Evaluation with YOLOv8, ArcFace, and SVM in a Contactless Employee Attendance System** (Terampe & Pramudwiatmoko, 2025) combined a YOLOv8 face detector with ArcFace embeddings and a support vector machine classifier for a workplace attendance system, evaluated on five employee classes. The authors reported an accuracy of 93.7%, precision of 0.938, recall of 0.937, and an F1-score of 0.935, and specifically noted that most misclassifications occurred between two employees whose facial features overlapped most closely, an observation that matches the general expectation that recognition errors concentrate on the most visually similar enrolled pairs rather than occurring uniformly at random.

## 2.5 Identified Research Gap

Across the reviewed attendance-system studies, a consistent pattern emerges: recognition accuracy is reported, sometimes at figures above 95%, but none of the reviewed attendance systems combine that recognition step with any liveness or anti-spoofing check at all. Alniemi and Mahmood (2023) reported strong detection and recognition accuracy without describing any check for whether the presented face was live. Thalor and Gaikwad (2024) likewise verified detected faces against a stored database with no liveness step of any kind. Terampe and Pramudwiatmoko (2025) evaluated their YOLOv8 and ArcFace pipeline purely on recognition accuracy, again with no anti-spoofing mechanism reported. This means that, as reported, a photograph of an enrolled person would satisfy each of these systems just as readily as the person's real presence, which is precisely the proxy-attendance weakness these systems otherwise set out to solve. Conversely, the reviewed liveness-detection literature is not built or evaluated as part of a complete attendance workflow at all: Keresh and Shamoi (2024) evaluated their transformer-based liveness model against standard spoof-detection benchmarks rather than inside a working attendance system, and Kuznetsov et al. (2025) reached a similar position in their survey, explicitly noting that no current liveness method achieves complete robustness.

The gap this project addresses is the integration of both halves into one evaluated system: a face recognition pipeline built from proven pretrained components, combined with a basic but real challenge-response liveness check, with both halves measured and reported together rather than only the recognition accuracy being reported in isolation. This project does not claim to solve liveness detection more robustly than the specialized literature reviewed above; it claims only to test whether a lightweight version of it, integrated directly into an attendance workflow and evaluated honestly, meaningfully closes the specific proxy-attendance gap that the reviewed attendance systems leave open.

## 2.6 Summary

This chapter reviewed the conceptual distinction between face detection, recognition, and verification, the role of convolutional neural networks and learned embeddings in modern face recognition, and the transfer learning approach that makes reusing a pretrained encoder practical for a project of this scope. It reviewed the theoretical basis for distance-threshold-based recognition decisions in the triplet-loss framework introduced by Schroff et al. (2015), then reviewed six recent studies spanning attendance-specific recognition systems and liveness-detection research. The comparative analysis showed that reviewed attendance systems report recognition accuracy without a liveness check, while reviewed liveness-detection research is not evaluated as part of a complete attendance system, identifying the combination of both, evaluated together, as the gap this project addresses.
